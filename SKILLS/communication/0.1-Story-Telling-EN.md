# AI-PhD.SKILL: Storytelling in AI Papers

[← Index](../../README.md) · [中文原文](0.1-Story-Telling.md) · English

I would roughly divide storytelling into two directions: solid and novel.

## Solid: making the reader think, “Now I see why.”

When walking during the day, an experienced person might tell you to avoid reflective patches on the road, because they are probably puddles. That seems like solid advice, until you go out at night and discover that it fails completely. You step right into every puddle.

If you submit a paper saying, “We propose a new algorithm for avoiding puddles at night, with a 15% improvement in accuracy,” reviewers may roll their eyes. You still have not answered the most important question: why does the method that works during the day fail at night?

A solid approach starts by getting down and looking at the light. During the day, sunlight from above reflects off the water into your eyes, making the water brighter than the road. At night, with weak ambient light, the rough road scatters light diffusely and can appear brighter than the mirror-like puddles. Now the rule makes sense: choose the dark patches during the day and the bright ones at night.

Only then is the motivation established. The method follows naturally: address the reversal in road-puddle reflectance at night with an adaptive threshold or a two-mode decision rule. The experiments should show more than a few points of improvement over a baseline. They should show that the earlier method really fails under nighttime reflection ambiguity, and that your method removes those errors in that setting.

The satisfaction of reading this kind of paper comes when the introduction makes the reviewer think, “So that is the root cause of the earlier method's failure.”

## Novel: making the reader think, “You can do that?”

There is another, more ambitious approach. Instead of discussing how to distinguish puddles at night, you say, “We invented rain boots.”

That is novelty. The reviewer's first reaction is surprise at what has become possible. You have changed the premise of the problem: why must we avoid puddles at all? Could we make them irrelevant?

This story is much harder to tell. A solid paper must withstand attacks on its logic; a novel paper must establish its necessity. In very little space, you have to show that rain boots exist and offer something more fundamental, general, and elegant than developing night vision. You are in trouble once the reviewer starts thinking that the boots are too heavy, work only in shallow water, or do not serve people who simply want better night vision.

So the introduction cannot merely describe the material the boots are made of. It must explain why footwear was previously overlooked, what technical breakthrough makes the boots feasible now, and whether wearing them makes the entire puddle-avoidance subproblem disappear.
