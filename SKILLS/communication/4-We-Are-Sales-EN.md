# AI-PhD.SKILL: We Are All in Sales

[← Index](../../README.md) · [中文原文](4-We-Are-Sales.md) · English

I increasingly feel that everyone in research does some form of **selling**.

You propose an idea and hope your advisor will support it. You invite a colleague to collaborate and hope they will invest time. You write a paper and hope reviewers will accept your claims. You give a talk and hope the audience understands and remembers the work. You apply for an internship, project, or faculty position and hope others believe in your ability and potential.

All these situations involve **helping others understand your value and giving them a reason to buy in**.

By sales, I mean a basic ability to explain value, present evidence, and reduce the effort others need to understand, trust, and support your work. Exaggeration is unnecessary.

## 1. Ideas do not speak for themselves

There is a Chinese saying that gold will always shine.

But gold does not generate light. It reflects it.

A piece of gold needs real value, and it also needs light to reach it, people to see it, and someone to recognize it correctly. If it stays buried or mixed into a pile of stones, most people have neither the ability nor the obligation to dig it out.

Research is similar. A good idea, paper, or result does not automatically attract attention. It needs a clear title and abstract, a coherent story, an effective presentation, and appropriate communication and publicity.

The same work can be described as:

> We improved performance on a benchmark by 1.5%.

Or:

> Existing methods fail systematically on an important class of examples. We identified the cause and developed a low-cost solution.

The experiments may be identical, but the information the reader receives is different. The first description gives a result. The second gives the problem, weakness, insight, solution, and value.

Much of research competition also happens in **how value is defined and communicated**.

What problem does your work solve? Why does it matter? Why are existing methods insufficient? What new information does your observation provide? Why should others use, cite, or build on it?

If you do not explain these things, readers must find the answers themselves. Most will not invest that much extra effort in unfamiliar work.

**Do not leave the entire job of identifying your work's value to others.**

## 2. Do not reject your idea on the market's behalf

These thoughts are common in research:

- The idea does not seem impressive enough.
- The method looks too simple.
- The improvement seems too small.
- Others may not be interested.
- A large group has probably done it already.

The author starts retreating before the experiments have established what the idea can do.

Caution matters, but premature dismissal prevents promising work from developing. Most research ideas begin incomplete: a phenomenon, a guess, a small change, or a vaguely perceived problem.

Their value often emerges through experiments, analysis, and explanation.

An ordinary-looking idea may reveal a stable failure pattern. A simple method may show that earlier approaches were unnecessarily complicated. A small average gain may concentrate on the most important hard cases. An engineering change may substantially lower deployment costs.

Do not reject the idea on behalf of your advisor, reviewers, and the community merely because it is not dazzling at first glance.

**Your job is to test it carefully and present it in its strongest honest form.**

Others can then decide whether to buy in.

## 3. Research needs publicity too

For many projects, communication stops when the paper is finished. Authors assume the community will notice once the paper and code are online.

In reality, people face a flood of new papers every day. Attention is scarce. Strong work can remain unknown for a long time without effective communication.

Research therefore needs PR too.

That can mean a clear title and abstract, an understandable talk, a post summarizing the work, reaching out to relevant researchers, releasing usable code, creating an intuitive demo, or presenting findings in the right setting.

The purpose is simple: **make it easier for the right people to see, understand, and use your work**.

Good PR focuses on reaching the right audience effectively. It does not require fabricated excitement or endless repetition.

- Which researchers is the work relevant to?
- Which community's problem does it address?
- What is the one conclusion people should remember?
- Which format best demonstrates its value?
- What can a reader do next?

The core of PR is creating a shorter path between valuable content and the people who need it.

Some researchers dislike publicity because introducing their own work feels self-serving. But clear communication is part of research. It helps others discover results sooner, reduces duplicated effort, and can lead to discussions, citations, and collaborations.

The purity of gold determines whether it has value. Lighting and placement determine whether anyone sees it.

**Research quality sets the potential value. Communication helps that value become visible.**

## 4. Good sales requires understanding the other person

Effective communication begins with understanding what the audience cares about.

An advisor may care about research value, feasibility, and time. A potential collaborator may care about an interesting problem, clear responsibilities, and reliability. Reviewers care about valid claims, fair comparisons, and sufficient evidence. A talk audience wants to know how the problem relates to them and what they can take away.

The same idea therefore needs different emphasis for different audiences.

**You can change the emphasis. You cannot change the facts.**

Choose a relevant entry point and organize the material clearly. Do not manufacture a conclusion that your evidence cannot support merely because the audience would like it.

Good sales helps people understand the problem quickly, recognize its value, and trust the evidence.

Without understanding, they cannot follow. Without value, they see no reason to care. Without evidence, they cannot trust the result.

A paper is, in this sense, a complete set of sales materials. The title and abstract attract the right readers. The introduction builds initial buy-in. Related work locates the contribution. The method explains the solution. Experiments supply evidence. Ablations and analysis address doubts. Limitations define boundaries. Rebuttal further reduces the reviewer's uncertainty in making a decision.

When writing, consider both what you did and **why readers should care and believe it**.

## 5. Simple ideas need clear explanations

People sometimes add complexity to a simple method because they worry it does not look novel enough.

But if a simple method works, simplicity may itself be valuable.

Explain why this was not done before, which overlooked problem it addresses, when it works, whether the results are stable, and whether simplicity improves efficiency, interpretability, or reproducibility.

For simple methods, emphasize:

- **Whether the problem insight is accurate.**
- **Whether the solution is necessary.**
- **Whether the benefit justifies the cost.**

Complexity does not automatically buy novelty, and simplicity does not imply a lack of contribution. Many influential methods are straightforward. They identify an important problem and establish the facts clearly.

Good communication lets others see that a simple method solves an important problem, has strong evidence, and is inexpensive to use.

## 6. Promote actively without overclaiming

Sales and PR have clear boundaries.

You can choose a persuasive angle, but evidence must support the claim. Success on one dataset does not establish universal effectiveness. Testing one model does not establish model independence. If gains mostly come from more compute, report that cost. If the method improves only some cases, state its scope. If experiments show correlation, do not present it as causation.

The higher you push the claim in the title and abstract, the more evidence your experiments must provide. If the evidence cannot support it, the entire story quickly loses credibility.

The approach I trust over the long term is:

**Be proactive about value and restrained about evidence.**

Explain why the work matters and show its strongest value, while accurately representing its capabilities, scope, and limitations.

Packaging a project to hide unreliable results is no better than selling a defective product. Selective reporting, hiding failures, unfair comparisons, unclear data provenance, inflated personal contributions, and grand narratives that conceal methodological weaknesses all erode credibility.

**Trustworthiness** is the underlying asset in research sales. Exaggeration may attract attention once. Reliable work builds a lasting reputation.

The healthier order is: **make the work good, explain it clearly, and help the right people find it**.

## Putting it into practice

We are all in sales. We introduce ideas to advisors, collaborators, reviewers, audiences, and the community, hoping they will understand the value and buy in.

Do not retreat just because an idea looks ordinary. Test it carefully, identify the problem it actually solves, explain the most valuable part, provide credible evidence, and communicate it to relevant people.

Keep the boundaries clear: do not invent value, conceal costs, turn local findings into universal laws, or let the rhetoric outrun the evidence.

**Sell confidently, communicate proactively, and claim carefully.**

Will gold always shine? Gold reflects light. Research quality determines whether you are holding gold; communication determines whether the light reaches it.
