# AI-PhD.SKILL: Reward Shapes Policy

[← Index](../../README.md) · [中文原文](3-Reward-Policy.md) · English

In any system, understand **what gets rewarded** before deciding where to put your effort.

Reward shapes policy. Whatever a system consistently rewards, its participants will gradually learn to optimize.

But reward is often only an approximation of real value. Many rules come from decisions made higher up about what can be measured, audited, compared, and implemented.

Understanding the rules matters. Letting them determine everything you do is dangerous.

My own principle is:

**Use the rules to guide short-term tactics, and long-term goals to choose your direction.**

## 1. Understand the reward before investing effort

I first felt this strongly while preparing for recommendation-based graduate admission, a route in China that allows students to enter graduate study without the usual entrance examination.

I had assumed that GPA and mathematics competitions mattered most, so I spent much of my effort improving my grades and preparing for competitions.

Only when the final admission ranking was calculated did I discover how many other achievements could earn points.

The benefit of working hard to move my GPA from 3.9 to 4.1 might have been smaller than that of planning ahead for a couple of provincial competitions, research projects, or other eligible achievements.

That was a shock:

**Sometimes effort does not produce a corresponding return because you did not first understand the reward function.**

Effort matters, and so does its direction.

- Recommendation-based admission rewards a composite ranking.
- Scholarships reward achievements that can be quantified in an application form.
- Paper submission rewards contributions that reviewers can understand and recognize.
- Internships and graduate recruiting reward role fit, project experience, referral connections, and interview performance.
- PhD training rewards an ability to identify problems, research output, communication, collaboration, and accumulated expertise.

Without understanding what the system rewards, you may use the wrong policy to optimize an imagined reward.

The painful part is that you may be working very hard while receiving little positive feedback.

## 2. Rules are often proxies

Understanding reward matters, but **the rules are not the value itself**.

Many rewards are imperfect proxies for complex goals under practical constraints.

Take graduate-admission rules. The underlying goal may be to identify broad ability, learning ability, research potential, and consistency. Those are difficult to measure directly, so they are compressed into GPA, competitions, papers, honors, and other countable items.

These indicators are meaningful, but cannot fully represent a person.

Similarly, good research seeks important problems, reliable methods, and valuable discoveries. Reviewers have limited time, however, and conferences must review at scale. They therefore look for clear claims, adequate baselines, credible experiments, and a complete argument.

Those standards are necessary, yet cannot capture all research value.

In hiring, an employer wants to know whether a candidate will solve real problems in the future. The process must make decisions within limited time, so résumés, projects, interviews, referrals, and alignment with the role become visible signals.

Those signals are useful approximations.

A reward function often has two properties at once: it affects your outcome, and it may diverge from what truly matters.

Both can be true.

## 3. Do not become a greedy algorithm for reward

Once you understand the rules, you can fall into the opposite trap: optimizing them greedily.

Do whatever earns the most points. Do whatever gets noticed. Do whatever gives the highest immediate return. Do whatever can quickly be packaged as an achievement.

That may work in the short term. Over time, it can become reward hacking.

In graduate admission, chasing bonus points can displace foundational skills. In research, chasing popular benchmarks can erode your own problem judgment. In publication, following only the forms reviewers like can produce complete-looking but limited work. In recruiting, collecting résumé keywords can distract from the ability to solve problems.

This is the danger of a proxy: when optimized too aggressively, it can drift away from what it was meant to measure.

I now try to look at two layers:

**The system's reward** determines how you obtain opportunities, rankings, jobs, acceptances, and recommendations in your current environment.

**Your long-term goals** determine who you want to become, which abilities you want to build, and which problems you want to solve.

Focusing only on long-term goals can underestimate real selection pressures. Focusing only on the system's reward can let short-term metrics shape you.

It is better to keep both in view.

## 4. Let rules guide tactics and goals guide direction

This principle matters a great deal to me:

**Use the rules to guide short-term tactics, and long-term goals to choose your direction.**

When preparing for graduate admission, read the policies early. Learn which achievements count, which competitions are worth the effort, and which materials need advance preparation.

If you want a research career, mathematical foundations, reading English, coding, communication, and research taste still deserve investment. They may not all earn points immediately, but they influence how far you can go.

When submitting a paper, understand the target community's preferences. Communities differ in how they value novelty, benchmarks, analysis, systems, theory, and applications.

If you want to do good research over the long term, you cannot rely entirely on trends, leaderboards, or easy stories. Identifying worthwhile problems and judging research require sustained practice.

When looking for a job, understand the job description, interview structure, available roles, referral routes, and timeline.

If you want to become a strong researcher or engineer, actual ability, trustworthiness as a collaborator, clear expression, and continued learning matter more over time.

Rules help you pass the current stage more effectively. Long-term goals explain why you want to pass it and where you intend to go afterward.

## 5. Questions to ask when entering a system

Before entering a new system, I now ask:

- What does it reward explicitly?
- What does it reward implicitly?
- What gets counted?
- What matters but is not directly counted?
- Which rewards are proxies for real ability?
- Which proxies are easy to overoptimize?
- Which short-term outcomes do I need at this stage?
- Which long-term abilities must I avoid sacrificing?

These questions can prevent a lot of wasted effort.

They also help distinguish **metrics the current system requires you to optimize**, such as deadlines, rank, benchmarks, reviewer concerns, and role fit, from **abilities you need to build over time**, such as foundations, writing, coding, communication, taste, collaboration, judgment, and credibility.

The first group shapes short-term outcomes. The second shapes long-term potential.

## 6. Understanding rules is not opportunism

There is a boundary worth making explicit.

Understand rules to make effort more effective. Use them to avoid needless friction. Respect them to preserve trust.

Pursuing only short-term reward can easily become opportunism: exaggerating contributions, producing empty achievements, claiming experience you did not earn, inflating a résumé, or manufacturing false signals. These may bring immediate benefits while damaging long-term credibility.

Research and professional development share an important property:

**Credibility is a long-term asset.**

What you do today can come back to you years later.

The attitude I prefer is to understand the rules without treating them as infallible, use the rules without breaking them, and optimize short-term rewards without sacrificing long-term goals.

## Putting it into practice

Reward shapes policy. People optimize what a system rewards, so understand that reward before investing heavily.

But rewards are often proxies shaped by practical constraints. They may be incomplete or imperfect representations of real value. A purely greedy response to the rules is therefore risky.

A steadier approach is:

**Understand the reward, design your policy, and protect your long-term goals.**

Before working, identify the rules. While working, respect them. Afterward, examine whether the wrong reward pulled you off course.

This is something I have felt increasingly strongly during my PhD: maturity means understanding the system of rules you face while staying clear about what you actually want to optimize.
