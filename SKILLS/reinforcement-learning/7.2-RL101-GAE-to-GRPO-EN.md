## 02 | What does this chapter address?

Article 7.1 obtained update weights for individual tokens:

$$
R\longrightarrow G_t\longrightarrow \widehat A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}}.
$$

Article 7.2 asks three further questions:

1. **Advantage estimator:** where does $\widehat A$ come from?
2. **Policy update:** given $\widehat A$, how far can the policy move in one update?
3. **Weighting:** how much weight does each prompt, response, and token receive in the loss?

We will use this breakdown:

| Layer | Representative methods |
| --- | --- |
| Advantage estimator | GAE, GRPO |
| Update constraint / granularity | TRPO, PPO, GSPO |
| Sampling / reduction / reward | Dr.GRPO, DAPO |

A training pipeline can combine methods from different layers. Algorithm names become meaningful when mapped back to their role in the loss.

## 03 | From GAE to the actor loss

The most direct actor loss is:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{PG}}(\theta)
=-\widehat{\mathbb E}_t\!\left[
\operatorname{sg}(\widehat A_t)
\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)
\right].
$$

- $\widehat A_t>0$: increase this token's probability.
- $\widehat A_t<0$: decrease this token's probability.
- Stop gradients through $\widehat A_t$ during the actor update.

If each rollout batch receives only one update before immediate resampling, this loss is already sufficient.

LLM rollouts are expensive. We would like to split a batch into minibatches and train for several epochs. After a few updates, the current policy $\pi_\theta$ has moved away from the data-generating policy $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$. We must therefore account for policy mismatch.

## 04 | Importance sampling

The general importance-sampling identity is:

$$
\mathbb E_{z\sim p}[f(z)]
=\mathbb E_{z\sim q}\!\left[\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}f(z)\right].
$$

The current batch was generated by $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$, while training uses $\pi_\theta$. Define the token ratio:

$$
\boxed{
\rho_t(\theta)
=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}
{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}
=\exp(\log p_t-\log p_t^{\mathrm{old}})
}.
$$

Implementations usually store the old log-probability of each sampled token and recompute its new log-probability during training.

An exact change of distribution over full trajectories involves a product of ratios, which can have high variance. PPO instead holds fixed the state distribution sampled by the old policy and uses token-level ratios to construct a local surrogate.

## 05 | Why is the surrogate reliable only nearby?

With the ratio, we can write:

$$
L^{\mathrm{sur}}(\theta)
=\widehat{\mathbb E}_{t\sim\pi_{\mathrm{old}}}
[\rho_t(\theta)\widehat A_t].
$$

Immediately after the batch is generated:

$$
\pi_\theta=\pi_{\mathrm{old}},\qquad \rho_t=1.
$$

At that point, the surrogate gradient aligns with the target policy gradient. After repeated updates:

- The new policy visits a different distribution of prefixes and states.
- $\widehat A_t$ still comes from the old rollouts and old critic.
- The policy may overexploit inaccurate advantages in a finite sample.
- One parameter update changes the probabilities of many tokens at once.

We therefore want a region within which to trust each policy update: a trust region.

## 06 | TRPO: using KL to limit a step

TRPO optimizes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_\theta\quad &
\widehat{\mathbb E}_t[\rho_t(\theta)\widehat A_t]\\
\text{s.t.}\quad &
\widehat{\mathbb E}_{s_t\sim\pi_{\mathrm{old}}}
\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(
\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\Vert
\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)\right)\right]\le\delta.
\end{aligned}
$$

The surrogate supplies the direction; the KL constraint limits the step.

Practical TRPO uses a linear approximation to the objective and a second-order approximation to KL, estimates $F^{-1}g$ with conjugate gradient, and then performs a line search. A theoretical performance bound motivates the design, while neural-network training also involves sampling and local approximations.

The geometry is clear, but the implementation is substantial. We would like a similar goal with an ordinary first-order optimizer, which brings us to PPO.

Reference: Schulman et al., 2015, *Trust Region Policy Optimization*.

## 07 | PPO-Clip

PPO-Clip uses a clipped surrogate:

$$
\boxed{
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)
=\widehat{\mathbb E}_t\!\left[
\min\!\left(
\rho_t\widehat A_t,
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat A_t
\right)\right]
}.
$$

The actor minimizes $-L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}$.

- There is no need to explicitly solve a second-order problem with a KL constraint.
- Adam or SGD can be used.
- The same rollouts can support minibatches and multiple epochs.
- Movement too far in the improving direction reaches a plateau in the objective.

TRPO directly constrains average KL. PPO-Clip modifies the surrogate to provide a simple first-order alternative. The original PPO paper also discusses adaptive KL penalties; the objective commonly called PPO today usually refers to PPO-Clip.

Reference: Schulman et al., 2017, *Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms*.

## 08 | When is the policy gradient actually zero?

A common implementation is equivalent to:

$$
\ell_t=\max\!\left(
-\widehat A_t\rho_t,
-\widehat A_t\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)
\right).
$$

To determine whether the clipped branch is selected, consider both the sign of the advantage and the direction of the ratio:

| Condition | Selected branch | Gradient of the PPO PG term with respect to $\rho_t$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\widehat A_t>0,\ \rho_t>1+\epsilon$ | clipped | 0 |
| $\widehat A_t>0,\ \rho_t<1-\epsilon$ | unclipped | retained |
| $\widehat A_t<0,\ \rho_t<1-\epsilon$ | clipped | 0 |
| $\widehat A_t<0,\ \rho_t>1+\epsilon$ | unclipped | retained |

A ratio outside the interval is therefore not enough to establish a zero gradient. The plateau occurs only on the side that moves too far in the direction favored by the advantage.

The zero here refers specifically to this token's PPO policy-gradient term. Entropy, KL, other losses, other tokens, and shared-parameter updates can still change its probability.

Code check noted in the original: OpenAI Spinning Up, Baselines, and the verl DAPO recipe all form unclipped and clipped terms and select the more conservative branch element by element.

## 09 | Multiplicative symmetry of the ratio

The importance ratio is multiplicative:

$$
\rho=\frac{p_{\mathrm{new}}}{p_{\mathrm{old}}},
\qquad
\text{swapping old / new gives }\frac1\rho.
$$

PPO commonly uses an additively symmetric interval:

$$
[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon].
$$

If the interval must remain unchanged when old and new are swapped, a multiplicatively symmetric form is:

$$
\boxed{\rho\in[1/c,c]}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|\log\rho|\le\log c.
$$

For a lower bound fixed at $0.8$:

$$
[0.8,\,1/0.8]=[0.8,1.25],
$$

while PPO with $\epsilon=0.2$ gives $[0.8,1.2]$. Conversely, fixing the upper bound at $1.2$ gives a reciprocal lower bound of $1/1.2\approx0.833$.

This distinguishes additive symmetry from multiplicative or log symmetry. It does not establish an optimal clipping rule on its own; branch selection still depends on the advantage sign. DAPO uses $[0.8,1.28]$, with an upper bound above the reciprocal-symmetric value $1.25$, to serve its exploration objective.

## 10 | Three policies, two kinds of KL

| Object | Role |
| --- | --- |
| $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ | Generates the current rollout; denominator of the ratio |
| $\pi_\theta$ | The policy currently being trained |
| $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ | Usually a frozen SFT or reference model |

The first kind of proximity controls the distance of an update from the rollout policy:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\mathrm{old}}\Vert\pi_\theta).
$$

The second limits divergence from the reference model:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).
$$

The first belongs to the TRPO/PPO update-control problem. The second is regularization often added in LLM RL. $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ does not enter the PPO importance ratio. Reference KL may be retained or removed depending on the training setting.

The cycle for one batch is:

$$
\text{rollout}\to
\text{freeze old logprobs / }\widehat A
\to K\text{ epochs update}\to
\text{resample}.
$$

## 11 | Why does GRPO remove the critic?

Classic PPO commonly uses a critic and GAE:

$$
V_\phi(s_t)\longrightarrow\widehat A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}}.
$$

In LLM RL for mathematics, the critic adds costs:

- A value head or a value model of comparable scale is needed.
- A value must be predicted for every token.
- Sparse outcome rewards make prefix-level values difficult to learn.
- Training actor and critic together adds memory and system complexity.

We can sample $G$ responses to the same prompt. They all address the same question, so their rewards supply a natural comparison: which responses are above or below the group average?

GRPO replaces the learned prefix-level critic with sampled group statistics for constructing advantages. The theoretical concepts of $V$, baseline, and advantage remain. The training signal shifts from value prediction to empirical comparison among repeated rollouts for the same question.

## 12 | The GRPO objective

For the same prompt $x$, sample:

$$
y_1,\ldots,y_G\sim\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(\cdot\mid x),
\qquad R_1,\ldots,R_G.
$$

The group-relative advantage for outcome-based GRPO is:

$$
\boxed{
\widehat A_i=
\frac{R_i-\bar R}{\operatorname{std}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)},
\qquad \widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i
}.
$$

We then retain token-level PPO ratios and clipping:

$$
L_{i,t}=\min\!\left(
\rho_{i,t}\widehat A_i,
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat A_i
\right).
$$

The original DeepSeekMath GRPO averages tokens within each response and adds a reference KL penalty.

From PPO to GRPO, the advantage estimator changes from critic plus GAE to group-relative outcome reward. The policy optimizer retains PPO-style token ratios and clipping.

Reference: Shao et al., 2024, *DeepSeekMath*.

## 13 | Four choices inside a short formula

The GRPO objective contains four kinds of design choices.

### Reward scaling

$$
R_i-\bar R
\quad\text{or}\quad
\frac{R_i-\bar R}{\sigma_R}.
$$

### Loss reduction

$$
\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_tL_{i,t},\qquad
\frac1{\sum_i|y_i|}\sum_{i,t}L_{i,t},\qquad
\frac1{L_{\max}}\sum_tL_{i,t}.
$$

### Proximity granularity

Compute a separate ratio and clipping decision for each token, or share one decision across the sequence.

### Batch construction

Do all-correct and all-incorrect groups enter the batch? What reward does a response receive if it is truncated at the maximum length?

Later methods modify different parts:

- Dr.GRPO: reward normalization and the loss denominator.
- DAPO: clipping, sampling, reduction, and overlong rewards.
- GSPO: importance ratios and clipping granularity.

## 14 | Dr.GRPO

Dr.GRPO stands for GRPO Done Right. The paper identifies two implicit reweightings in original GRPO.

### Response-level length bias

$$
\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_tL_{i,t}
$$

gives responses comparable total weight. Tokens in short positive responses receive stronger updates, while each token in a long negative response receives a weaker penalty. This may encourage incorrect responses to grow longer.

### Question-level difficulty bias

$$
\frac{R_i-\bar R}{\sigma_R}
$$

makes the overall gradient scale for a prompt depend on its sampled pass rate or reward variance, producing dynamic prompt reweighting.

Dr.GRPO changes these choices to:

$$
\widehat A_i=R_i-\bar R,
\qquad
\text{loss denominator}=L_{\max}\ \text{or another global constant}.
$$

The response mask still determines which tokens are valid. Each response's own length no longer supplies the denominator. The paper describes the change as restoring an unbiased estimator for its objective.

Reference: Liu et al., 2025, *Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training*.

## 15 | DAPO: why can Clip-Higher resist entropy collapse?

For a sampled token with $\widehat A>0$, PPO encourages its probability to rise until the ratio reaches the upper clipping bound.

With $\epsilon=0.2$:

$$
0.9\to1.0:\quad \rho=1/0.9\approx1.11<1.2,
$$

a high-probability token can approach 1 without triggering the upper clip.

$$
0.2\to0.3:\quad \rho=0.3/0.2=1.5>1.2.
$$

A low-probability token increasing by only $0.1$ already has a large relative change and reaches the plateau sooner. The clipped branch is selected, and this token's PPO PG term has zero gradient with respect to $\rho$.

Existing high-probability patterns can therefore become more concentrated, while rare but correct exploration tokens have less room to rise. The sampling distribution becomes sharper and entropy falls.

DAPO decouples the bounds:

$$
[1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},\,1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}],
\qquad \epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}>\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}}.
$$

The paper uses $(\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}})=(0.2,0.28)$. For an old probability of $0.2$, the limit up to which the objective continues to improve rises from $0.24$ to $0.256$. A move from $0.2\to0.3$ is still clipped, but the additional room helps preserve exploration and entropy.

Reference: Yu et al., 2025, *DAPO*.

## 16 | DAPO's other three components

### Dynamic Sampling

If all responses to a prompt are correct or all are incorrect, their centered advantages are zero. DAPO continues sampling and filters out these groups so that the training batch retains enough useful prompts.

### Token-Level PG Loss

$$
\frac{1}{\sum_i|y_i|}\sum_{i,t}L_{i,t}.
$$

A batch-wide token average gives each valid token comparable weight. Longer responses therefore have greater total contribution. This and Dr.GRPO's fixed-budget denominator correspond to different objectives.

### Overlong Reward Shaping

A length penalty increases near the generation limit, reducing the abrupt reward change and training noise caused by truncation.

DAPO's long-CoT recipe also removes reference KL. That choice serves the paper's rule-based-verifier setting and needs reassessment when transferred to other RLHF settings.

The four techniques address entropy collapse, uninformative groups, length weighting, and truncation noise. DAPO's effectiveness comes from the recipe working together.

## 17 | GSPO

In GRPO, rewards and advantages are at the sequence level, while importance ratios and clipping are at the token level. GSPO moves the update unit to the whole sequence as well.

Define a length-normalized sequence ratio:

$$
\boxed{
s_i(\theta)
=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}
{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left[
\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_t\log\rho_{i,t}
\right]
}.
$$

Clip the entire response together:

$$
\min\!\left(
s_i\widehat A_i,
\operatorname{clip}(s_i,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat A_i
\right).
$$

Tokens in a response share one sequence-level multiplier. The geometric mean controls the numerical scale associated with length. GSPO retains group-normalized advantages; its central changes are the importance ratio and clipping unit.

Because the ratio definitions differ, numerical $\epsilon$ values are not directly comparable between GSPO and token-level GRPO. The paper also reports stability benefits concerning MoE expert-routing volatility and differences between training and inference likelihoods.

Reference: Zheng et al., 2025, *Group Sequence Policy Optimization*.

## 18 | What does each method change?

| Method | Advantage | Update control | Reduction / unit | Main purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TRPO | GAE and other estimators | Average KL constraint | Timestep | Principled trust region |
| PPO | Commonly critic + GAE | Token ratio clipping | Implementation-dependent | First-order updates across multiple epochs |
| GRPO | Group-standardized reward | Token ratio clipping | Response token mean | Remove the critic |
| Dr.GRPO | Centered group reward | Token ratio clipping | Fixed budget | Address length and difficulty bias |
| DAPO | Group-relative reward | Asymmetric token clipping | Global token mean | Exploration and a practical training recipe |
| GSPO | Group-relative reward | Sequence ratio clipping | Sequence objective | Align the reward and update units |

The full route:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{GAE}&:\ \text{estimate token advantages}\\
\mathrm{TRPO}&:\ \text{control the policy step with KL}\\
\mathrm{PPO}&:\ \text{construct a first-order surrogate with clipping}\\
\mathrm{GRPO}&:\ \text{replace the critic with group comparisons}\\
\mathrm{Dr.GRPO/DAPO}&:\ \text{address weighting and training failure modes}\\
\mathrm{GSPO}&:\ \text{move the update unit to the sequence}
\end{aligned}
$$

When evaluating a new RL optimizer, ask in order: where does $\widehat A$ come from? How are old and new policies accounted for? At what granularity are updates limited? What is divided by what in the final reduction?
