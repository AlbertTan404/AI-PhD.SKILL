# AI-PhD.SKILL: RL 101, Understanding PPO and GRPO Through a Loss

[← Index](../../README.md) · [中文原文](7-RL101.md) · English

Today I want to talk about RL, a topic where I have long occupied an awkward position: I know what it is, but have less understanding of why it works, and even less of how to make it work.

I have recently been going through autumn recruiting, and many teams want people who “understand RL.” I increasingly think that understanding has at least four levels.

**Level 0:** You can reproduce the GRPO formula from memory and use a framework to get training running.

**Level 1:** You understand every term. You know what $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$, $\pi_\theta$, and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ represent, why we compute a ratio, why we clip, and what GRPO removes compared with PPO.

**Level 2:** You can work from the objective through policy gradients, baselines, critics, TD, GAE, PPO, and GRPO. You understand which gap in the previous loss each new concept addresses.

**Level 3:** You can understand why a complete RL system works and diagnose why it fails. The issue may lie in the task, rollout, verifier, advantage, sampling, loss reduction, truncation, exploration, or held-out evaluation. At this level, you can explain why DAPO uses Dynamic Sampling, Clip-Higher, Token-Level Loss, and Overlong Reward Shaping, instead of memorizing four more names.

For a long time, I stayed at Level 1. I had seen GRPO's formula many times and recognized most symbols. But if someone asked why the advantage was computed that way, why a critic was needed, where GAE fitted into PPO, or why clipping helped reuse the same data, my answers became vague.

So I wanted to work through it from the beginning.

This article is for you if you:

- Have a rough idea of GRPO.
- Understand LLMs, log-probabilities, and gradient descent.
- Want a reasonably solid, systematic understanding of RL, from policy gradients through TD and GAE, including the Markov property, and on to PPO, GRPO, and DAPO.
- Would rather not begin with dozens of pages of equations.

If you are already an expert in mathematics or RL, or have no background at all in probability, gradients, or LLM training, this may not be the right fit.

Let's begin.

## 1. What are we trying to optimize?

Take LLM RL for mathematics. Given a problem $x$, the model generates a response $y$. A verifier checks the answer and returns a final reward $R(x,y)$. Start with the simplest setting: 1 for a correct answer, 0 for an incorrect one, no intermediate rewards, and $\gamma=1$.

The objective is:

$$
\max_\theta J(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}
[R(x,y)].
$$

In plain language, we want the model's future responses to receive a higher average score.

If we treat the entire response as one action, this is a contextual bandit: receive a prompt, sample a response, and obtain a reward.

But an LLM generates one token at a time:

$$
\pi_\theta(y\mid x)
=
\prod_t \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t),
\qquad
s_t=(x,y_{<t}),\quad a_t=y_t.
$$

We can therefore unfold the same problem into a token-level decision process. The state is the problem plus the generated prefix; the action is the next token.

Everything that follows addresses one question:

**How do we turn one final verifier reward into a loss that updates each token's probability?**

## 2. How can we train an LLM with a nondifferentiable reward?

The verifier returns only 0 or 1. It is usually nondifferentiable and cannot tell the model which reasoning step went wrong.

But the probability the model assigns to its response is differentiable.

Policy gradients connect the reward to the gradient of log-probability:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\mathbb E\left[
R(x,y)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)
\right]
=
\mathbb E\left[
\sum_t R(x,y)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)
\right].
$$

We can construct a sampled surrogate loss:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{PG}}(\theta)
=
-\widehat{\mathbb E}
\left[
\sum_t w_t\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)
\right].
$$

When computing the actor gradient, the sampled tokens and weights $w_t$ are treated as fixed data. We do not differentiate through the reward or sampling process.

The simplest choice is $w_t=R(x,y)$.

For a correct response, the update encourages higher log-probability for all sampled tokens. With a zero reward, an incorrect response supplies no direct gradient of its own. If rewards can be negative, low-reward trajectories can also be directly discouraged.

This is REINFORCE.

We already have a valid algorithm for training an LLM with RL. Its variance may be large, sample efficiency low, and credit assignment coarse, but the algorithm is complete.

The starting point is straightforward: **sample a trajectory and weight its log-probability by its reward**.

One problem remains. A difficult question and a trivial question both give 1 point for a correct answer. Should those two points cause exactly the same update strength?

## 3. From absolute score to improvement over expectation

Raw reward tells us what score we obtained. We would like to ask:

**Starting from this prefix, how much better was the outcome than expected?**

Introduce a baseline:

$$
\hat A_t^{\mathrm{MC}}
=
G_t-b(s_t).
$$

Here $G_t$ is the realized cumulative return from the current token onward. With only a terminal reward and $\gamma=1$, every generated token has $G_t=R$.

Suppose this rollout scores 1, but the expected score from this prefix was 0.6. The update weight is $1-0.6=0.4$.

If the rollout scores 0 when the expectation was 0.6, the weight is $-0.6$, encouraging a lower probability for the sampled trajectory.

As long as the baseline does not depend on the current action $a_t$ given $s_t$, and is stop-gradient in the actor loss, it does not change the expected policy gradient:

$$
\mathbb E_{a_t\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)}
\left[
b(s_t)\nabla\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)
\right]
=0.
$$

A baseline need not be perfect or even be a neural network. Choices include a constant, a batch reward mean, or the mean reward of other responses to the same prompt, excluding the current response.

If we want it to vary with the problem and current prefix, we can train a critic:

$$
V_\phi(s_t)
\approx
V^\pi(s_t)
=
\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid s_t].
$$

It answers: **if we continue generating from this prefix under the current policy, what final score should we expect?**

The most direct training method uses the actual rollout return $G_t$ as a label, moving $V_\phi(s_t)\longrightarrow G_t$.

We now have two learning tasks:

- The actor learns the next-token distribution to improve future reward.
- The critic learns the expected return of the current prefix to provide a better comparison point for the actor.

Keep this distinction clear: **in $G_t-V_\phi(s_t)$, critic inaccuracy primarily affects variance and does not itself bias the policy gradient. Dependence on critic accuracy becomes more consequential when we bootstrap.**

## 4. Why Bellman equations, TD, and GAE?

We can train a critic from complete outcomes. But a response may contain thousands or tens of thousands of tokens, with reward available only at the end. That supervision is sparse and has high variance.

We would like a more local relationship: estimate the current prefix's value from this step's reward plus the next prefix's value.

For that to work, the current state must contain enough information to describe future transition and reward distributions. This is the Markov property: given the current state and action, the future should not depend on earlier history omitted from the state.

The Markov property does not require storing the full history. The state needs to be a sufficient statistic of the history for predicting the future. For plain text generation, including the prompt and full generated prefix in $s_t$ is the natural choice.

We can then write the Bellman expectation equation:

$$
V^\pi(s_t)
=
\mathbb E_\pi
\left[
r_t+V^\pi(s_{t+1})\mid s_t
\right].
$$

Treat the right-hand side as a revised estimate after observing this step, and subtract the previous estimate. This gives the TD error:

$$
\delta_t
=
r_t+V_\phi(s_{t+1})-V_\phi(s_t).
$$

For LLM generation with only a terminal reward:

- At nonterminal tokens, $r_t=0$, so $\delta_t=V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)$.
- At the last step, we receive the reward and set the terminal state's value to zero, giving $\delta_t=R-V(s_t)$.

For an intermediate token, TD asks: **after generating this token, does the critic think the probability of eventually answering correctly has gone up or down?**

If $V_\phi=V^\pi$, the conditional expectation of $\delta_t$ given $s_t,a_t$ equals the true advantage: $\mathbb E[\delta_t\mid s_t,a_t]=A^\pi(s_t,a_t)$.

Real critics are imperfect. Unlike a state-only baseline, $V(s_{t+1})$ depends on the current action through the next state. Its prediction error does not automatically cancel. TD trades lower variance for critic approximation bias.

We now have two extremes:

- **Monte Carlo:** wait until the episode ends and use $G_t-V(s_t)$. There is no bootstrap bias, but variance is high.
- **One-step TD:** observe one real reward and immediately bootstrap from $V(s_{t+1})$. Variance is lower, but dependence on the critic is stronger.

GAE interpolates between them:

$$
\hat A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}}
=
\delta_t
+\lambda\delta_{t+1}
+\lambda^2\delta_{t+2}
+\cdots.
$$

We still take $\gamma=1$. In the general form, the coefficient is $(\gamma\lambda)^l$.

- $\lambda=0$ gives one-step TD.
- $\lambda=1$, with a complete episode and a correctly zeroed terminal value, telescopes to $G_t-V(s_t)$.
- $0<\lambda<1$ mixes different horizons of real rewards and bootstrapped predictions.

Thus, with $\lambda=1$, even an imperfect critic leaves us with a Monte Carlo return minus a state-only baseline. With $\lambda<1$, critic error remains in the advantage estimate through bootstrapping.

GAE has a specific place in training: **it computes the update weight $\hat A_t$ for each token in the actor loss**.

We now have advantages. But we are still most comfortable taking just one update on these rollouts. Why?

## 5. Why do we need PPO?

Suppose the responses were generated by $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$. Before the first update, the current policy $\pi_\theta$ is identical to it.

After a few updates, $\pi_\theta$ has changed, while the data still comes from the old policy. Repeatedly treating that data as fresh samples from the current policy can produce increasingly aggressive updates.

PPO records the old log-probability of each generated token and computes a probability ratio:

$$
\rho_t(\theta)
=
\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}
{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}
=
\exp(
\texttt{new\_logprob}_t-
\texttt{old\_logprob}_t
).
$$

The PPO-Clip actor loss is:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)
=
-\widehat{\mathbb E}_t
\left[
\min\left(
\rho_t\hat A_t,
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t
\right)
\right].
$$

Set aside the full formula for a moment.

If $\hat A_t>0$, the sampled token was better than expected, so we want to increase its probability. Once the ratio exceeds $1+\epsilon$, further increases receive no additional benefit from this term.

If $\hat A_t<0$, we want to decrease its probability. Once the ratio falls below $1-\epsilon$, further decreases receive no additional benefit either.

Clipping therefore **removes the extra incentive to move too far in the direction that improves the surrogate objective**. It does not force every ratio to remain inside an interval.

Shared parameters and interactions between token gradients can still push ratios outside the clipping range. PPO does not guarantee monotonically increasing real reward. It supplies a relatively conservative local surrogate that allows multiple rounds of minibatch updates on the same rollouts.

Within one PPO iteration, keep these fixed:

- The rollout policy $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ that generated the batch.
- The recorded `old_logprobs`.
- The critic values recorded at rollout time.
- The advantages and value targets computed once from that batch.

Only the current actor $\pi_\theta$ and critic $V_\phi$ change during training. After several PPO epochs, sample a new batch with the updated actor.

Distinguish another common symbol, $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$:

- $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ is the snapshot that generated the current batch and is refreshed for the next iteration.
- $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the anchor for KL regularization, fixed within an optimization stage. Common implementations use the initial or SFT model; some algorithms refresh it in an outer iteration.
- The denominator in the PPO ratio is $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$, not $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

**GAE determines how to estimate advantages. PPO determines how to update the policy using those fixed advantages.**

## 6. From PPO to GRPO

PPO is useful, but a critic is expensive.

For LLMs, the critic is often similar in size to the actor. At a minimum, it requires an additional value head, activations, optimizer state, and training compute. Learning values for long chains of thought can also be unstable.

Can we avoid training a critic?

Group Relative Policy Optimization, or GRPO, samples several responses to the same prompt and constructs update weights from their relative rewards.

For $G$ responses to a problem $x$, with rewards $R_1,\ldots,R_G$, the group-relative advantage of response $i$ is:

$$
\hat A_i
=
\frac{R_i-\operatorname{mean}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)}
{\operatorname{std}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)}.
$$

A response above the group mean receives a positive advantage; one below it receives a negative advantage. Broadcast that sequence-level advantage to the response's tokens, then use a PPO-style ratio and clipped objective.

Along this route:

- PPO commonly uses a learned critic and GAE to obtain token-level $\hat A_t$.
- GRPO uses relative rewards from multiple responses to the same prompt to obtain $\hat A_i$.
- PPO-style mechanisms such as ratios, clipping, and an old policy remain.
- A critic and GAE are no longer required.

The original GRPO objective also directly includes a KL penalty relative to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. This regularization and the group-relative advantage are separate design choices: $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ constrains policy drift, while $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ supplies the importance-ratio denominator.

GRPO does more than save the critic's cost. Comparing responses to the same question naturally removes part of the difference in question difficulty. If a hard question occasionally produces one correct response, that response receives a much higher weight than the incorrect responses in its group.

But several problems remain:

- Multiple responses per prompt make rollouts expensive.
- An all-correct or all-incorrect group has no reward variance. With common numerical safeguards, its advantages become zero, yielding no useful policy gradient from that signal.
- Broadcasting a sequence reward to every token still does not identify precisely which reasoning step created value.
- Results depend heavily on the verifier, sampling distribution, length handling, and loss reduction.

GRPO replaces a particular route to advantage estimation within PPO. It is not a universal upgrade to every part of PPO.

## 7. Why DAPO becomes easier to understand

Decoupled Clip and Dynamic Sampling Policy Optimization, or DAPO, is instructive because it starts from actual training failures and changes four different parts of the RL pipeline.

**First, Clip-Higher addresses exploration.**

Standard PPO commonly uses a symmetric interval $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$. For low-probability tokens with positive advantage, the same ratio ceiling permits only a small absolute probability increase. The incentive to increase them can disappear early. DAPO decouples the lower and upper clipping bounds, allowing more room for upward movement. The paper's empirical explanation is that this helps preserve low-probability exploration tokens and mitigates entropy collapse in its training setting.

This is not a universal theorem that larger clipping bounds are better. Wide bounds can also destabilize updates. The point is that stability and exploration trade off, and upward and downward movement may not need symmetric constraints.

**Second, Dynamic Sampling addresses the effective batch.**

GRPO depends on relative rewards within a group. If every response to a prompt is correct, or every response is incorrect, the standard deviation is zero and the group cannot supply a group-relative gradient.

DAPO keeps sampling and filtering out those groups until the batch contains enough useful prompts with both correct and incorrect responses. This requires more rollouts but reduces uninformative samples in each optimizer step and stabilizes the effective batch size.

It also changes the training prompt distribution. Learning concentrates around the current boundary of the model's ability. Dynamic Sampling can therefore be viewed as a policy-dependent curriculum as well as an engineering choice.

**Third, Token-Level Policy Gradient Loss addresses loss reduction.**

A common reduction in original GRPO averages token losses within each response and then averages across responses. Responses receive equal weight, so an individual token in a long response receives less weight than one in a short response.

DAPO averages over all valid response tokens in the batch. Tokens receive more consistent weight. Long, high-quality reasoning is less diluted, and long, low-quality, repetitive, or garbled responses can be penalized more fully.

Moving a `mean` changes the relative weight assigned to different sequence lengths in the optimization objective.

**Fourth, Overlong Reward Shaping addresses truncation noise.**

A response that is wrong because the model cannot solve the problem differs from one that receives zero because it exceeds the maximum length and is cut off. Treating both as identical failures introduces noise.

DAPO first observes improved stability from filtering truncated samples, then uses a soft penalty that increases near the length limit, giving the model gradual feedback before hard truncation.

According to the paper's experiments and explanation, these changes address four practical pathologies:

- Exploration is suppressed too early.
- The batch contains fewer and fewer useful groups.
- Loss reduction weights long and short responses unevenly.
- Hard truncation creates noisy rewards.

This is what I mean by Level 3: asking **where the training signal disappears, becomes distorted, or receives the wrong weight**.

## 8. Connecting the full chain

Each concept addresses a gap left by the previous loss.

We want to update LLM parameters from final rewards, so we introduce policy gradients.

Absolute rewards in basic REINFORCE have high variance, so we introduce baselines and advantages.

We want a prefix-dependent baseline, so we train a critic to predict $V(s_t)$.

Complete Monte Carlo returns arrive late and have high variance, so we use the Markov property and Bellman equation for TD bootstrapping.

One-step TD depends heavily on the critic, while Monte Carlo has high variance, so GAE mixes estimates with different horizons.

Repeatedly updating on the same rollouts can move too far, so PPO uses an old-policy ratio and clipping to construct a local surrogate.

An LLM critic is expensive and may be hard to learn, so GRPO uses relative rewards from multiple responses to the same prompt instead of a learned value baseline.

At scale, exploration, useful sampling, length weighting, and truncation remain problematic, so DAPO changes clipping, sampling, reduction, and reward shaping.

The chain can be condensed into six roles:

1. **Policy gradients:** turn reward into a gradient.
2. **Baselines:** make update signals relative to a comparison point.
3. **Critics, TD, and GAE:** estimate advantages.
4. **PPO:** reuse fixed advantages for relatively conservative policy updates.
5. **GRPO:** obtain a baseline without a critic.
6. **DAPO:** address failures that remain in large-scale, long-CoT training.

Understanding RL means starting from the objective and knowing which problem each estimator, ratio, clip, mask, and reduction addresses, along with the tradeoffs it introduces.

I am still far from being an RL expert. But the next time I encounter an algorithm, I hope to ask more than how its formula differs from GRPO:

- Which training signal did it change?
- Which bias or variance did it reduce?
- Which samples received different weights?
- Which failure mode did it address?
- Does held-out evaluation show that the model learned the capability we actually wanted?
